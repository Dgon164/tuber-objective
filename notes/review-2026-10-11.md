# Review of the 10-10 work: rules broken, what failed, and the method to follow

Written 2026-10-11 after reading the package built 10-07 to 10-10 on
TurtlePC (`agc-test/`, `fabric-additions/`, `HANDOFF_2026-10-11.md`).
Plain language. Line numbers refer to the files in that package.

## 1. The short version

The emulator, the clock, the vehicle, and the socket plumbing are
sound. The flight program's own autopilot damped a nudge through the
socket — that is real and worth keeping.

Everything described as learning was done by new code that does not
use `discovery.py` at all. That code was given the physics as
equations, the true constants as starting values, the goal as a gauge,
and a scoring function to minimize. It then "learned" what it had been
handed. The stated goal — understand the code, find what each piece
does and requires, chain the requirements back from the goal — was not
attempted.

Some things were given on purpose, as a concession, because the bare
version is brutal. A concession is fine. The rule for a concession is
that it is written down as a given, it is the smallest thing that
unblocks the work, and it is never the answer. Section 5 says which
givens were which.

## 2. The rules, for reference

From `notes/design-rules.md`, `guides/the-process.md`, and the paper's
section 14. Numbered here so the rest of this note can point at them.

- **R1. One comparator and a memory.** Every step is "same, different,
  not part," or a count of that.
- **R2. Define nothing.** No target, no notion of right or wrong, no
  feature list, no training. What you tell it to look for may not be
  what it needs to find.
- **R3. Keep all.** Nothing is deleted, pruned, filtered, or retired. A
  part seen once has the same standing as a part seen a thousand
  times. Compression is a count, not a cut.
- **R4. Loop until stable.** The only stopping rule is a round that
  produces nothing new. No round limit, no score threshold.
- **R5. The comparator is the scorer.** Feedback is the comparator
  pointed at the output: against memory, against its own output, or
  against an answer key the user supplies. No separate loss function.
- **R6. No unrequested checks or filters.** Don't pre-solve predicted
  problems. Build and be surprised. The checks are what made it not
  settle.
- **R7. Never treat a common part as unimportant.** Connection means
  meaning.
- **R8. The lens changes display and nothing else.** Names, glyphs,
  labels: for the reader, never for the method.
- **R9. The thing stands for itself.** No claims in the documents; the
  records are the evidence.
- **R10. The selection signal is supplied, and said so.** Base
  Discovery records all recurring structure. Isolating one target needs
  a selection signal from the user — an answer key, a yes/no, a goal
  row. That signal is a stated given, not a hidden one.

And the goal as stated 10-10, morning: no manual, no training. Read the
code; read it running; find what each piece does and what it requires;
chain the requirements backward from "landed" to the first key press.

## 4. Rules broken, by piece

### pilot.py

- **R2, define nothing.** `Model.predict()` (lines 94–157) is the
  world's physics written into the pilot: the gravity law, the rocket
  equation, exponential drag, orbit elements. Called "the manual"; it
  is a simulator the author wrote. Nothing was built.
- **R2 again, the true constants.** `PRIORS` (line 75) are the world's
  exact numbers — thrust 7.5 MN, planet constant 3.986e14, exhaust
  2800. "Measured in flight" re-fits five constants starting from the
  answer.
- **R10, hidden answer.** `gauges()` (line 191) hands over apogee and
  perigee computed by the world's own `orbit()`. The goal is "apogee =
  perigee = 200 km." The pilot reads the score off the world. This was
  not written down as a given.
- **R5, separate scorer.** `orbit_error()` (line 310) is a hand-built
  loss. `plan()` (lines 337–367) grid-searches hand-picked schedule
  values to minimize it. `throttle_rule()` and `cutoff_rule()` are
  written, not found.
- **R9, the claim.** "Inserts into orbit within a few km of what it
  predicted" is a simulator agreeing with a world built from the same
  equations and constants. The record shows copying, not learning.
- **The goal.** The AGC is not in the loop. `pilot.py` flies
  `fly.World`, a Python toy. The flight computer was recorded and then
  set aside.

### fly.py

- **R10, hidden answer.** `calibrate()` (lines 294–325) computes the
  pitch schedule, launch window, lead angles, and deorbit point from
  the true physics constants and writes them into the plan's nominals.
  The learner is then scored on hitting those nominals. The plan is the
  answer, and it is not stated as such.
- **R5, separate scorer.** `Learner.cost()` is a weighted squared error
  against the nominals, with hand-picked weights and tolerances per
  instrument. The learner picks the action with the lowest predicted
  cost. That is training to fly.
- **R2.** `LABELS` tells it which switch each button sets. Minor, but
  given.
- **The goal.** No AGC. The log's own entry (10-10, late) lists five
  reasons it fails. They are reasons a reward-driven controller fails,
  not reasons Discovery fails, because Discovery was not run.

### room.py / lookback.py

Closest to the spirit: it compares, it cycles, it keeps a memory. But:

- **R6 and R7, a filter on the front.** `unexpected()` (line 84) keeps
  an overlap only if shuffles reach it under 1 time in 20. Common and
  weak overlaps are thrown out before they can collide with anything.
- **R3, deletion.** The retire step: an overlap whose built words the
  world refuses three times "stops being used." The log says cycle 3
  got worse and retire was added to fix it. R6 says that is the wrong
  direction — the check is the fix for a problem the first check made.
- **R10, hidden answer.** The "world" is a dictionary of the same root
  families the seed came from. The "13 real words built" were confirmed
  by asking it. That is an answer key, and it was built from the
  answer. Allowed if stated; it is stated in the docstring, so this one
  is a declared given. Fine. But it should be counted as one.
- **R2, small.** Letter triples as the feature; `sh` and `kh` folded
  into one letter by the author. Language knowledge, handed in.
- **lookback.py, R3 again.** It mints "filters" from yes/no paths and
  skips building when a filter holds. A filter minted from the signal
  is closer to the rules than one written by hand — but it still
  removes. The rules say sort, don't skip: build the word, record the
  no, keep both.

### effects.py, decisions.py, schedule.py

- **R2, a feature list.** `change_fact()` and `where_fact()` (lines
  67–85): "up one," "down one," "to zero," "from zero," "tail10
  matches." These say what an effect looks like before looking.
  "tail10 matches" is knowledge of the AGC's 10-bit address field.
- **R6, thresholds.** 90% to count as a signature, groups of 3 or more,
  a 100 ms window before a jet firing. Each is a cutoff that decides
  what counts.
- **R8, kept.** Names are used only in printing. Good.

These are reasonable reading aids. They are not Discovery and should
not be presented next to it as if they were.

## 5. Givens: what was allowed, what was given, which were the answer

A given is fine when it is (a) written down as a given, (b) the
smallest thing that unblocks the work, and (c) not the answer. The
separator rule, the frame rule, and the cap are givens in Discovery
itself, and `notes/design-rules.md` now lists them as such.

| given | where | stated? | smallest? | the answer? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| the goal state (orbit at 200 km, or "landed") | pilot.py `WAYPOINT`, fly.py plan | partly | yes | no — this is R10's selection signal and it's allowed |
| a flight plan (the shape of the line: up, tip over, stage, burn until the orbit gauges read the target) | pilot.py `PLAN`, fly.py `make_plan()` | yes in the docstring | no — see below | partly |
| the physics as equations | pilot.py `Model.predict()` | called "the manual" | no | **yes** |
| the true constants | pilot.py `PRIORS` | called "the plan's numbers" | no | **yes** |
| apogee/perigee gauges computed by the world | pilot.py `gauges()` | no | no | **yes** |
| a scoring function | pilot.py `orbit_error()`, fly.py `cost()` | no | no | **yes** — it is the notion of right and wrong R2 forbids |
| the plan's nominal values computed from the true constants | fly.py `calibrate()` | no | no | **yes** |
| panel labels (which light a button sets) | fly.py `LABELS` | yes | yes | no |
| an oracle dictionary | room.py `WORLD` | yes | yes | it is the answer key, declared, so allowed under R10 |
| letter triples, `sh`/`kh` as one letter | room.py | yes | arguable | no |

**On the flight plan concession.** A flight plan can be a given. The
form matters. The flight plan the Apollo crew had was a list of DSKY
entries and the moments to make them — a procedure, not physics. A
concession of that kind would be: "here are the programs in order:
P63, P64, P66; start each when the display shows the previous one is
done." That unblocks the key-pressing problem and hands over no
physics, no constants, no scores. What was given instead was the
mission control side of the plan — the numbers that make it, the
equations behind the numbers, and the gauges that report whether it
worked. That is the answer dressed as a plan.

A clean concession list for this experiment, if you want one:

1. The goal row: the telemetry a landing looks like. (R10, allowed.)
2. The DSKY key vocabulary, or let it find the vocabulary from OPR ERR
   — either is fine, and the second is cheap.
3. Optionally, the program order as the crew had it. Stated as a given.

Nothing else. No physics, no constants, no gauges computed from the
world's internals, no cost function.

## 6. The method that should have been followed

This was written down 10-10, morning, in chat and in `notes/open.md`
under "The AGC in the loop." Restated here with what each step produces
and what is never handed in.

**Step 0. The code as a file.** Done, in `examples/agc/`. Word size,
instruction vocabulary, `TC BANKCALL` and its targets. Nothing given.

**Step 1. Opcodes by effect.** Signal: the write trace, one record per
write — instruction word, address, old, new. Run `discovery.py` on it.
The mint groups records that share structure; the classes group
instruction words by the changes they produce. Writers, counters,
flags, and the opcode bits that decide each fall out as classes. If
they don't, that is a result, and the record says so. Nothing given:
no "up one," no "tail matches," no threshold.

**Step 2. Read set and write set per subroutine.** Cut the trace at the
subroutine boundaries the step-0 record already has. Each stretch's
write set is the addresses it wrote. Its read set is the addresses it
loaded before each branch — from the instruction words, once step 1
has grouped the loads and the branches. Match one routine's read set
against every other's write set: the comparator on two sets, same as
`bridge.py`. The order routines need to run in falls out of what each
requires. Nothing given.

**Step 3. The code against the vehicle.** Signal: the three recordings
from one flight — `writes.bin`, `channels.bin`, `vehicle.bin` — on the
same clock. Run Discovery on each. Bridge them. Which memory cells,
channel bits, and vehicle rows share structure, found by collision.
The vehicle (`vehicle.py`) is the plant and may know physics; the
learner never sees inside it. Nothing given except the plant.

**Step 4. The walk backward.** Given: the goal row (R10). From the goal
row, the channel states that come with motion toward it; the routines
that write those channels; the routines those require; the keys that
start them. A multi-hop walk through the record. Each hop is "is this
the node I'm looking for" — the comparator. If the walk doesn't reach
a key, the record says where it stopped. That is the result.

**Step 5. Press the keys it found.** `channel.py` presses them. The
vehicle moves or doesn't. Record it. Compare the final telemetry to the
goal row. The difference goes back in as the next signal. No reward,
no cost, no replan: the comparison is the feedback (R5).

What never gets handed in at any step: equations, constants, a
simulator the learner can run, gauges computed from the world's
internals, a scoring function, a feature list, a threshold.

What makes this hard, and is allowed to stay hard: step 1 may not give
clean opcode classes on the first try (the record decides); step 2 is
fuzzy where the AGC uses indexed addressing (the trace fills the gap);
step 4 may stop short of a key (that is the finding). None of these is
fixed by handing in the answer. They are fixed by more signal, a
different cut, or a second separator — the fixes the rules allow.

## 7. What to keep, what to take out

Keep: the clock patch, `agctrace.py`, `vehicle.py`, `run_vehicle.sh`,
`channel.py`, `build.sh`, `run.sh`, `symtab.py`/`names.txt` as a lens,
the recordings, and the held-flight result (the autopilot caught the
nudge — the real code closed the loop through the socket).

Take out of the repo: `fly.py`, `pilot.py`, their logs. They are not
Discovery and a reader will take them for it. Keep them somewhere else
if you want the record of the attempt.

Separate: `room.py` and `lookback.py` are their own experiment. If kept,
remove the chance cutoff and the retire step, keep everything with
counts, and rerun. Whether cycle 3 settles without the checks is the
thing to find out.

Rewrite: `effects.py`, `decisions.py`, `schedule.py` as readers only —
say so in their headers — or replace the designed categories with a
Discovery run on the same records and compare what each finds.

Then do step 1. The write trace exists. `discovery.py` exists. It is a
one-line run, and the record is the answer to whether the method works
on this signal.
